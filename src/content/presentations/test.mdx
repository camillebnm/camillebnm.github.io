# Shape deformation

We want to deform a shape continuously:

$$
\mathcal{S}_0
\longrightarrow
\mathcal{S}(t)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal{S}_1
$$

Applications:

- Shape morphing
- Landmark-constrained deformation
- Shape editing
- Pairwise-to-any morphing

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Our goal

We want a deformation that is:

- **continuous in time**
- **invertible**
- **explicitly evaluable**
- valid at arbitrary positions and times
- compatible with implicit representations

Most importantly:

> No numerical integration during inference.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Applications

Interpolation · Pairwise-to-any · Shape editing

<Slide>

# Why not directly learn a deformation?

A direct mapping

$$
D : \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d
$$

can map the source shape to the target shape.

But it does not tell us:

$$
\text{What happens between } t=0 \text{ and } t=1?
$$

We need a continuous trajectory for every point.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Mathematical flows

A mapping

$$
\phi :
\mathbb{R}^d \times \mathbb{R}
\rightarrow
\mathbb{R}^d
$$

is a flow if:

$$
\phi(x,0)=x
$$

and

$$
\phi(x,t+s)
=
\phi(\phi(x,s),t)
$$

This second property is the **group law**.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Consequences

The group law gives us:

$$
\phi^{-1}(x,t)=\phi(x,-t)
$$

Therefore:

- trajectories cannot cross
- the deformation is invertible
- forward and backward motion are consistent
- arbitrary time intervals can be composed

</Slide>


<Slide>

# The non-stationary problem

Some deformations cannot be represented as flows in the original space.

<img
  src="/presentations/figure/stat_nonstat/stationary_nonstationary.svg"
  alt="Stationary versus non-stationary flow"
  class="medium-figure"
/>

Trajectories can cross in space at different times.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Lift time into the space

We instead work in space-time:

$$
\phi :
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
\times
\mathbb{R}
\rightarrow
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
$$

with

$$
\phi((x_0,t_0),t)
=
(x(t+t_0),t+t_0)
$$

Time becomes an additional coordinate.

</Slide>



<Slide>

# Implicit shape deformation

Let the initial shape be represented by:

$$
\mathcal{S}_0
=
\{x \mid g(x)=0\}
$$

We want an implicit function:

$$
f(x,t)
$$

representing the shape at every time.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Transporting the implicit function

Level sets must be preserved:

$$
f(
\phi((x_0,0),t),
t
)
=
g(x_0)
$$

Using the inverse flow:

$$
f(x,t)
=
g(
\phi((x,t),-t)
)
$$

So the flow directly transports the implicit representation.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Our main idea

Instead of learning a vector field and integrating:

$$
\dot{x}=V(x,t)
$$

we directly construct:

$$
\boxed{
\phi((x_0,t_0),t)
=
(
\psi_{t+t_0}
\circ
\psi_{t_0}^{-1}(x_0),
t+t_0
)
}
$$

where each

$$
\psi_t
$$

is a diffeomorphism.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Why does this work?

At zero time:

$$
\phi((x_0,t_0),0)
=
(x_0,t_0)
$$

And composition gives:

$$
\phi(
\phi((x_0,t_0),s),
t
)
=
\phi((x_0,t_0),s+t)
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\phi \text{ is a mathematical flow}}
$$

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Time-parameterized diffeomorphisms

We construct a family:

$$
(\psi_t)_t
$$

such that every:

$$
\psi_t :
\mathbb{R}^d
\rightarrow
\mathbb{R}^d
$$

is invertible.

We use a RealNVP-like architecture.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Time-parameterized coupling layers

<img
  src="/presentations/figure/coupling_layers/coupling_layers_ours.svg"
  alt="Forward coupling layer"
  class="half-figure"
/>

<img
  src="/presentations/figure/coupling_layers/coupling_layers_inverse_ours.svg"
  alt="Inverse coupling layer"
  class="half-figure"
/>

The inverse has essentially the same computational cost as the forward pass.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Coupling layer

Split:

$$
x=(x_I,x_{\bar I})
$$

and transform only part of the coordinates:

$$
\tilde{\psi}_t(x)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
x_I \\
s_\theta(x_I,t)
\odot
x_{\bar I}
+
v_\theta(x_I,t)
\end{pmatrix}
$$

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Exact inverse

Because the scaling is non-zero:

$$
s_\theta(x_I,t) > 0
$$

the layer is analytically invertible.

Therefore:

$$
\psi_t^{-1}
$$

is available directly.

No fixed-point iterations.

No ODE solver.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# EFIS

## Explicit Flow for Implicit Surfaces

Our architecture gives:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Flow}
=
\psi_{t+t_0}
\circ
\psi_{t_0}^{-1}
}
$$

Properties:

- Explicit
- Invertible
- Continuous
- Satisfies the group law
- Efficient forward and inverse evaluation

</Slide>



<Slide>

# Optimization

We optimize the flow using:

### Endpoint constraints

- Dirichlet loss
- Neumann loss
- Interior/exterior loss
- Landmark loss

### Intermediate-time regularization

- Isometry
- Volume preservation
- Temporal regularization

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Dirichlet loss

Surface points should reach the target surface:

$$
g_1(
\phi((x,0),1)
)
=
0
$$

and backward:

$$
g_0(
\phi((y,1),-1)
)
=
0
$$

This constrains the endpoints of the deformation.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Neumann loss

We also align normals.

This encourages:

- smooth surfaces
- consistent local geometry
- better intermediate shapes

The deformation is therefore constrained not only by position, but also by differential information.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Interior / exterior loss

Surface constraints alone are not sufficient.

<img
  src="/presentations/figure/pb_signe/signe.svg"
  alt="Sign ambiguity"
  class="medium-figure"
/>

We must also preserve which points are inside and outside the shape.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Landmark loss

Given correspondences:

$$
\mathcal{C}
=
\{
(x_0,x_1)
\}
$$

we enforce:

$$
\phi((x_0,0),1)
\approx
x_1
$$

Landmarks provide semantic information when it is available.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Intermediate-time regularization

Endpoint constraints alone do not control:

$$
\mathcal{S}(t)
\quad
\text{for }
0<t<1
$$

We regularize the entire trajectory.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Isometry

We encourage:

$$
J_{\psi_t}^T
J_{\psi_t}
\approx
I
$$

This promotes distance preservation.

It helps prevent:

- excessive stretching
- spurious motion
- geometric distortion

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Volume preservation

Local volume change is controlled by:

$$
\det(J_{\psi_t})
$$

We penalize:

$$
\log
\det(J_{\psi_t})
$$

to encourage:

$$
\det(J_{\psi_t})
\approx
1
$$

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Temporal regularization

We penalize rapid temporal variations:

$$
\mathcal{L}_T
=
\int
\left\|
\partial_t
\psi_t(x)
\right\|^2
\,dx\,dt
$$

This produces smoother trajectories.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Shape interpolation

$$

# Pairwise-to-any morphing

Suppose we have:

$$
\mathcal{S}_1,
\mathcal{S}_2,
\dots,
\mathcal{S}_N
$$

and a canonical shape:

$$
\mathcal{S}_0
$$

Instead of computing all pairwise deformations...

</Slide>


<Slide>

# The key idea

Compute only:

$$
N
$$

flows:

$$
\mathcal{S}_i
\longrightarrow
\mathcal{S}_0
$$

Then obtain every pairwise deformation by composition.

<img
  src="/presentations/figure/composition/composition.svg"
  alt="Pairwise-to-any composition"
  class="medium-figure"
/>

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Complexity

Naively:

$$
\frac{N(N+1)}{2}
$$

pairwise morphings.

With a canonical shape:

$$
\boxed{N}
$$

optimized flows.

All other transformations are obtained by composition.

</Slide>

# Limitations

### Topology preservation

A flow is invertible.

Therefore, EFIS cannot:

- create holes
- remove handles
- merge disconnected components
- split a shape into multiple components

The source and target must have compatible topology.

</Slide>


<Slide>

# Other limitations

- Network expressivity depends on architecture depth
- Optimization remains computationally expensive
- The choice of canonical shape affects pairwise-to-any morphing
- Explicit trajectories are especially useful for interpolation, but less important for standard generative tasks

</Slide>
